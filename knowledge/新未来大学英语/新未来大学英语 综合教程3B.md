Lecturer: Good afternoon, everyone. My name is Francis Windall. And I'm here today to talk to you about the effects of negative emotions. As we go through our day-to-day lives, we experience a wide range of emotions. On any given day, it is likely that the average person will have many positive feelings, such as happiness, excitement or pride, but also many negative emotions such as sadness, anger, fear or even despair. But what happens when we feel more negative emotions than positive ones? And what are the effects of not being able to process our negative emotions effectively?

There was an article about depression published in 1991 by the psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema. She said that spending too much time thinking about negative emotions can make someone's depression last longer. It can also give (them) a negative outlook on life, make people less motivated, and also interfere with their abilities such as problem-solving. Being unable to process your negative emotions can also have a bad effect on your relationships with others. An experiment led by Sacco showed that people with prolonged depression are more likely to experience rejection and a loss of social support.

The tendency to keep thinking about these negative emotional experiences is linked to a number of lifestyle choices and coping behaviors, such as drinking too much and overeating. These behaviors then have a negative impact on physical health. There is evidence to suggest that repeatedly thinking about our negative emotional experiences can itself result in poor physical health. Researchers have found that this kind of negative thinking can lead to high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease and even strokes.

Despite all the negative aspects of negative emotions, it is still very important that we acknowledge them. In fact, suppressing our emotions can be just as damaging as thinking about them too much. A 2013 study by Chapman found that people who suppressed their emotions were more likely to die early, and more likely to be diagnosed with cancer. So, while it's clear that there are many downsides to dwelling on our negative feelings, our health is also at risk if we do not dwell on them at all. Thank you very much.

Presenter: Welcome to "Keep in Mind", where we discuss the latest thinking in psychology. On today's show we're talking about negative emotions. With us is Dr. Arabella Nugent, a professor of psychology and the author of the book It's OK to Be Sad. Dr. Nugent, welcome to the show.

Dr. Nugent: Thank you for having me.

Presenter: Can you give us a brief summary of what you discuss in the book?

Dr. Nugent: Yes. I'm interested in how we deal with negative emotions. I think the first thing we have to do is to accept them. There's no point pretending that we're not feeling sad or angry. That will only make things worse. It's also important to remember that there are actually good reasons why we have negative emotions, from an evolutionary perspective. Fear, anger and disgust prepare us for flight, fight and avoidance respectively. Even sadness is a useful emotion, as it can help us to be less judgmental.

Presenter: So if acceptance is a first step, what methods are there for then dealing with negative emotions?

Dr. Nugent: There are a number of methods. One is "visualization". In order to avoid repeatedly thinking about negative emotions, try to do the following mental exercise regularly: Picture your best possible self in any given situation. How would you react? What would you do? What would you say? If you do this regularly, it can help you to be ready when negative emotions do start to affect you. Another technique is to practice active gratitude.

Presenter: Do you mean that we should be thankful that we are feeling angry or sad?

Dr. Nugent: No, not exactly. What I mean is that we should take time to think about the things, and the people, that are good in our lives. Every now and again we should express our gratitude for those things, or to those people. Of course, the way we express gratitude varies from culture to culture. People from some cultures, such as China and South Korea, tend to express gratitude by giving something back to the person they are grateful to, while other cultures favor verbal means of expressing gratitude. The important thing, however you express your gratitude, is that doing so can put you in a much more positive mindset, and that makes you more resilient.

Presenter: Fascinating advice, Dr. Nugent. Thank you.

Dr. Nugent: My pleasure.

OK, everyone. Today we're going to look at how to design a questionnaire for your research.

So, as we've talked about many times, the key to conducting any type of research is to narrow your focus. You need to be able to frame your study in very specific terms, and then write your questions. You'll basically be choosing between closed questions and open-ended questions. Generally, closed questions make it easier for you to collect and analyze your data, so you'll definitely want to use closed questions if you plan to survey a lot of people; otherwise, you'll have to read and interpret all the individual answers. However, open-ended questions may be the only way to get at individual responses beyond the limit of closed questions. But also for both you and the respondent, open-ended questions can help make the answers more engaging and informative.

Now, not only must you phrase your questions very carefully, but also consider the sequence of the questions you ask. Think about the experience of the respondent as they complete the survey: Logically ordered questions will make the questionnaire easier to complete; therefore respondents will be more likely to complete the survey.

All right. Now you've got a great variety of clear questions that are in the perfect order. But don't forget that at the beginning of your questionnaire you need to add a note about the purpose of your research and how the data will be used. Let's say you get this all done. You're ready to go, right? Hmm? Nope. Sorry.

Next you'll need to pilot your questionnaire before administering it in your study. Even the best-written questionnaire can benefit from rehearsal. And as the author, you will inevitably overlook some aspects of the questionnaire while writing it. These will come to light in the pilot.

A pilot also allows you to confer with respondents afterwards to gauge their understanding of the questions and get any feedback they may have. You can then use that feedback to revise the questionnaire as needed.

# Unit 4 Only One Earth

## Section 1 · Episode 1 · Audio · Scripts

Lecturer:

Welcome to our workshop on "Green Cities". Today, we'll find out about some cities that have successfully adopted green policies, and we'll ask what else can be done to make our urban areas more eco-friendly. First, though, I think it's important that we remind ourselves why we're here, by answering the question: "Why do we need 'green cities'?"

We have to remember that urbanization has increased drastically in the last 200 years. In the year 1800, only about 3 percent of the world's population lived in cities. By the year 2017, that number was roughly 55 percent. Experts predict that by the year 2050, as many as two-thirds of the humans on the Earth will be living in cities. As 1.5 million people are added to the global population every week, it is likely that our cities will continue to grow exponentially.

As you can imagine, the population increase places a great deal of pressure on the local environment. Expanding cities need more buildings and more infrastructure, for example, roads and supplies of water, gas and electricity. This causes more waste and more pollution, which in turn are a cause of substantial damage to the environment. Additionally, local wildlife is also put at risk, and there is a negative effect on people's health. Hello, everybody. Welcome to my lecture. I'm professor Dew Warrick. Today's lecture will cover four aspects: asthma in Australia, the causes of asthma, why asthma is more common in Australia and how to prevent asthma.

So, what are the statistics on asthma in Australia now?

From the data shown here, it's clear that Australia has an unusually high percentage of people with asthma. In fact, based on data from 70 countries, Australia was the only country in which over 25 percent of the population aged 18-45 were reported as having symptoms of asthma. In India, China and Mexico, for example, that figure was less than 10 percent.

So, what are the causes of asthma?

The exact causes are not known, but we do know some of the risk factors. The risk of getting asthma partly depends on genetics; we know that asthma can run in families. Also, household-related factors seem to be relevant. For example, children whose parents smoke seem to be more at risk. Another potential cause of asthma is the environment; we know that pollen and pollution in the air can be contributing factors. Then personal causes include obesity, or heavy physical training.

But why is asthma more common in Australia?

Although there isn't a single dominant explanation, there are some possibilities. Environmental factors may have a significant effect. In Australia, especially in Victoria, New South Wales and Queensland, the weather produces storms between September and January, when there is an increased pollen count in the air. The storms break the pollen into even smaller bits, with the danger that we breathe them in and trigger asthma attacks. The most serious example in modern times happened in November 2016 when Melbourne was hit by a bad storm and 8,500 people were rushed to the city's emergency rooms because they couldn't breathe properly.

Another factor could be the "hygiene hypothesis". This is the theory that when children are raised in an environment that is particularly washed and cleaned, they are not exposed to enough germs. As a result, their immune system does not have a chance to develop and become stronger, thus increasing the chance of the children developing illnesses like asthma. People in Australia have a habit of keeping things very clean and sanitized. This, in combination with the earlier mentioned environmental factors, might explain why Australia has so many asthma sufferers.

So how can we prevent asthma?

At the moment, there is no cure for asthma. But to prevent and ease the symptoms, inhaled therapy is the preferred method. However, young children can't manage their breathing when using an inhaler, so a bigger device with a face mask is recommended for them. Ok, that's being my talk on asthma today. I've been professor Dew Warrick. Thank you for listening to my lecture. Bye-bye.

The Belt and Road Initiative

Many people tend to think of globalization as a modern phenomenon. However, in reality, trade has been strengthening the economic connections between far-flung nations for thousands of years.

More than two thousand years ago, the diligent and courageous people of Eurasia explored and opened up several routes of trade and cultural exchanges that linked the major civilizations of Asia, Africa and Europe. These routes were collectively called the Silk Road by later generations. Commodities traded along the Silk Road included much more than silk. Gold, silver and spices were among the most valuable goods transported. Routes of the Silk Road were among the most significant and longest-lasting trade routes in the ancient world.

In 2013, the Silk Road started a new chapter when President Xi Jinping proposed to build the Silk Road Economic Belt and the 21st-Century Maritime Silk Road which together became known as the Belt and Road Initiative.

The Initiative is made up of two main components. By land, the Silk Road Economic Belt starts from China on the east end of the Eurasia continent, and passes through Central Asia, West Asia, South Asia and some other regions by three lines (north line, middle line, and south line). By sea, the 21st-Century Maritime Silk Road connects Chinese ports to Europe through the South China Sea and the Indian Ocean in one route, and to the South Pacific in the other.

The aim of the Initiative is to enhance regional economic cooperation, strengthen exchanges and mutual learning between different civilizations, and promote world peace and development. It is a great undertaking that will benefit people around the world.

The Belt and Road Initiative has seen fruitful results amid high-quality development. By January 2021 a total of 171 countries and international organizations had signed 205 cooperation agreements with China on the joint construction of the belt and road. It has not only brought increased development to China, but also provided a new level of prosperity and cooperation to countries along the belt and road. It is a shining example of mutual benefit through collaboration. As more partners jump on board this mutually beneficial journey, cooperation is becoming broader and more in-depth, and more and more countries stand to benefit from the Initiative.
